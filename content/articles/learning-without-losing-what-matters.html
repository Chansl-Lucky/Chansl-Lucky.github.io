A system that keeps learning needs a deliberate relationship with its past. Which knowledge should survive the next update? Which information should fade? And how should new preferences change its behavior? These questions connect two of our recent projects, PeCL and LifeAlign, and shape how we think about intelligence that improves over time.

Choosing what memory should preserve

In PeCL, published at EMNLP 2026, we study privacy in continual learning. A model accumulates information as it encounters successive tasks, and that information can include both useful general knowledge and sensitive details. Treating every token identically can make the tradeoff between privacy and utility unnecessarily difficult.

We introduce a dynamic, token-level differential privacy strategy that allocates privacy budgets according to semantic sensitivity. We then use that sensitivity information to guide memory sculpting: reducing the retention of sensitive information while preserving historical knowledge that remains useful across tasks. The paper evaluates how these components balance privacy protection with performance on previously learned tasks.

The research question is precise: can the learning process make better decisions about what it carries forward? PeCL addresses that question within its stated privacy model and experimental setting. Its results should be read with those assumptions in view.

Keeping alignment through change

LifeAlign studies a different challenge: maintaining alignment as a language model learns a sequence of preferences and domains. An update that helps with a new preference can erode behavior learned earlier. We investigate how to adapt while retaining useful alignment patterns over time.

Our approach combines focalized preference optimization with short-to-long memory consolidation. The optimization strategy adjusts learning intensity according to sample difficulty. The consolidation mechanism denoises task-specific updates and projects them into a stable memory space. Together, these components support adaptation to new tasks while reducing forgetting of earlier ones. The official implementation exposes the training and evaluation pipeline for further study.

LifeAlign was presented as an oral at AAAI 2026. Congratulations to our collaborators! The project makes lifelong alignment a concrete training and evaluation problem, with current-task learning and previous-task retention considered together.

Memory as a design decision

PeCL and LifeAlign tackle distinct objectives. One asks how sensitive information and useful knowledge should be handled during continual learning; the other asks how preference alignment can persist through a sequence of updates. We see a common design principle in this work: memory deserves an explicit objective, a mechanism for updating it, and evidence that the mechanism serves its purpose.

For future agents, we want improvement to be judged over a trajectory. A convincing evaluation should examine what an update adds, what it disrupts, and what it leaves behind. That perspective motivates our broader interest in continual learning and recursive self-improvement in real-world environments.
